\section{Measurement Regions}%
\label{sec:Regions}

This section presents the final event selection that is applied to define the measurement phase space.
Only events passing all selection criteria are considered in the definition of the fiducial cross section.
Two analysis regions are defined and summarized in Table~\ref{tab:RegionDefinitions}.

The selection is optimised using simulated MC event samples.
All events are required to contain exactly one signal lepton as defined in ....
Prompt leptonic \( \tau \) decays are considered signal in this analysis and therefore included.
Events are selected with a single lepton trigger and a minimum \pt requirement of \qty{30}{\GeV} is placed on the lepton.
At least three jets are expected in the signal process: two $b$ jets and at least one jet from the hadronic \Wboson decay.
The neutrino from the leptonic \Wboson boson decay introduces \met.
A cut is defined on \( \met + \mtW > \qty{60}{\GeV} \) to remove background from QCD events
with a mis-identified or non-prompt lepton.
The missing transverse mass \mtW is computed from
the lepton and \met 4-vectors where the \(z\)-component is set to 0.

The \textit{Loose} region defines the most general phase space constraining the interference
effects between \ttbar and \tW processes and improve the modeling over a large kinematic region.
The explicit reconstruction of the hadronic \Wboson boson introduced in the previous section is
exploited to define the \Wboson-hadronic region.
A mass window around the \Wboson mass is defined for the \Wboson candidate $(60~\GeV < m(\Whad) < 100~\GeV)$,
as well as a minimum \pt requirement.
These cuts suppress contributions from events with poorly reconstructed \Wboson candidates.
The loss in data statistics is compensated by a significantly enhanced background suppression.
This analysis region provides a clean data sample with small background suppression.
The explicit reconstruction of the \Wboson candidates further allows the construction of
pseudo-tops from \Wboson, lepton and the two leading $b$ jets in an event.
The observables measured in this region are valuable for development and improvement of MC models.
They will also be used to extract the mass of the top quark in Chapter....

A direct comparison of the two measurement regions is shown for the lepton \pt spectrum in Figure~\ref{fig:Regions:Leppt}.

A quantitative overview is provided in Table ... where the event yields for the two regions are listed.

\section{Unfolding}

The distributions measured at the detector level are distorted by a variety of experimental effects, including detector acceptance, reconstruction efficiency, energy and momentum resolution, as well as misreconstruction and misidentification of particles. 

In the \textit{unfolding} step, these detector-induced distortions are corrected to recover the underlying physical distributions from the observed data.
The differential particle-level cross sections that are obtained can be compared to test theoretical predictions probing the SM. All detector specific effects are removed, allowing the comparison of the results across measurements and experiments, as well as to future predictions which may not be available at the time of the analysis.

While the procedure of unfolding is conceptually straightforward, its practical implementation presents significant challenges. Various unfolding algorithms and software packages are available, but in this analysis, the TUnfold framework~\cite{Schmitt:2012kp} is employed due to its robustness and flexibility in handling complex detector responses.

A \textit{migration matrix} is constructed, which describes how events are redistributed across bins due to detector effects. This matrix is then inverted, adding a regularization term to stabilize the inversion process while avoiding overfitting. Every component of the systematic uncertainties is propagated individually through the unfolding procedure.

This chapter provides a comprehensive introduction to the unfolding methodology, detailing the theoretical foundations, the required input ingredients, and the practical challenges involved. It then outlines the specific unfolding strategy applied in this analysis, including the construction of the migration matrix, the choice of regularization and the propagation of uncertainties.

\subsection{Unfolding procedure and cross section definition}

The measurement is performed as a counting experiment, where events are grouped into certain 
regions of phase-space, also called bins.
The definition of the differential fiducial cross section as a function of an observable $X$
in a given bin $i$ is given by the formula
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma_\text{fid}}{dX_i} = \frac{1}{L\cdot \Delta X_i} \cdot \frac{1}{\epsilon_i} \cdot \sum_j M_{ij}^{-1}f_{\text{acc}}^j(N_{d,j}-N_{b,j}).
    \label{eq:Unfolding}
\end{equation}
with the integrated luminosity $L$ and the bin width $\Delta X_i$.
Corrections are applied for the acceptance $f_{\text{acc}}^j$ and the efficiency $\epsilon_i$, respectively.
The migration matrix is denoted $M_{ij}$ and its inverse is used to correct for the event migration between bins.
$N_{d,j}$ is the number of data events in bin $j$, while $N_{b,j}$ is the number of background events in the same bin.

The first step subtracts the background from the data.
The $N_{b,j}$ are obtained from data driven methods or MC simulations, as explained in Section~\ref{sec:background}.
The remaining events are considered as signal consisting of \ttbar and $tWb$ events.

The response matrix $M_R$ keeps tracks of event migration.
Particle level events generated in bin $i$ that end up in bin $j$ after the detector simulation
are filled in $M_{R,ij}$. An ideal measurement would therefore produce a perfectly diagonal matrix.
Due to the limited resolution in real-world experiments, off-diagonal elements may also be filled.
The migration matrix $M_{ij}$ is obtained when normalizing the response matrix
\begin{equation}
    M_{ij} = \frac{M_{R,ij}}{N_{t,i}},
\end{equation}
where $N_{t,i}$ is the number of particle level (true) events in bin $i$.
Thus, $M_{ij}$ can be interpreted as the probability of a particle level event generated in bin $i$ to be reconstructed in bin $j$.
The choice of the bin boundaries is such that the diagonal elements of the matrix contain at least 60\% of particle level events per row.

A simple inversion of $M$ in order to obtain the true particle level distribution is
not sufficient, as it can enhance statistical fluctuations in the data.
Therefore, a regularization term is added which ensures a dampening of this effect
and therefore a smooth unfolded distribution.
This analysis employs the Tikhonov regularization scheme~\cite{Tikhonov1995}
as implemented in the TUnfold package~\cite{Schmitt:2019um}.

A least squares method with Tikhonov regularization and additional
area constraint is employed to estimate the true distribution. 
The strength of the regularization parameter $\lambda$ is determined in a \textit{L-curve scan}~\cite{lCurve}. The regularization term is plotted against the least-squares term of the data, schematically shown in Figure~\ref{fig:unfolding:migma:lcurvescan}. 
The point with the largest curvature defines the value of $\lambda$, which is typically small $\lambda \ll 1$.

A regularization mode that penalizes the absolute size of the unfolded bins.
is chosen in this analysis. It suppresses large fluctuations, while smoothness between neighboring bins is not enforced explicitly.

The number of degrees of freedom in the minimization has to be larger than zero.
Therefore, the number of bins on reconstructed level is chosen to be twice as large, compared to the particle level distribution.
The bin edges coincide, adding an intermediate bin on detector level to ensure convergence and a smooth distribution
at reconstruction level.

Only those events that pass both the particle and reconstructed-
level selection are described by the migration matrix.
Therefore, two other distributions need to be taken into account.

The acceptance $f^j_\text{acc}$ is defined as the fraction of all events passing the detector-level selection that are also matched to particle-level, i.e. events that pass-particle and detector-level cuts. A low acceptance indicates that the detector-level selection is too broad relative to the particle-level definition, leading to a mismatch between the observed and true physical phase space.
It can be calculated from the response matrix as 
\begin{equation}
    f^j_\text{acc} = \sum_i M_{R,ij}/N_{d,i}.
\end{equation}

The complementary efficiency 
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_i = \sum_j M_{R,ij}/N_{t,i}
\end{equation}
is defined as the fraction of matched events out of all events passing the particle-level 
selection. It corrects for events that fail to be reconstructed or selected at the detector
level. This can result from limitations in detector coverage, insufficient resolution,
trigger thresholds or reconstruction algorithms.

\subsection{Propagation of uncertainties}

Uncertainty components associated with the cross section measurement are
propagated from detector level through the unfolding pipeline to unfolded cross
section level. Deviations from the nominal results are evaluated separately for
the upward and downward variation. 
The single uncertainties are assumed to be uncorrelated.
The propagated uncertainties are therefore summed up in quadrature
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_\text{tot}^\text{up/down} = \sqrt{\sum_\text{sys} (\sigma_\text{sys}^\text{up/down})^2}
\end{equation}
Depending on the nature of the uncertainty, different propagation procedures are
used, as described in the following. 
The systematic uncertainty is defined as the shift in the cross section
where the nominal result is taken from Equation~\eqref{eq:Unfolding}
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_\text{sys} = \frac{d\sigma_\text{nom}}{dX} - \frac{d\sigma_\text{sys}}{dX}
\end{equation}
The signs of the cross section shifts are kept.
Up and down variations are expected to be symmetrical around the nominal result.
One-sided uncertainties are symmetrized on detector level.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Detector systematic uncertainties}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
Detector systematics are propagated from detector to particle level by calculating varied unfolding corrections, i.e. acceptance, efficiency, migration matrix and background predictions
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma_\text{sys}}{dX_i} = \frac{1}{L\cdot \Delta X_i} \cdot \frac{1}{\epsilon_i'} \cdot \sum_j {M_{ij}^{-1}}'
    {f_{\text{acc}}^{j}}'(N_{d,j}-N_{b,j}').
\end{equation}
Dashed quantities indicate the systematic variation.
The nominal data is unfolded with these varied corrections and the difference to the nominal cross section defines the uncertainty.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Signal modeling}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
Uncertainties in the signal modeling are implemented employing pseudo-data obtained from MC simulations. They incorporate signal and background processes $N_{pd} = N_s + N_b$.
Varying migration matrix and correction factors in the unfolding of the pseudo data returns the systematically shifted cross section
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma_\text{sys}}{dX_i} = \frac{1}{L\cdot \Delta X_i} \cdot \frac{1}{\epsilon_i'} \cdot \sum_j {M_{ij}^{-1}}'
    {f_{\text{acc}}^{j}}'(N_{pd,j}-N_{b,j}').
\end{equation}
which is then compared to the nominal result.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Background modeling}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
Uncertainties in the background modeling are propagated by varying the background prediction, while keeping all other unfolding corrections fixed and reapplying the unfolding procedure to the data
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\sigma_\text{sys}}{dX_i} = \frac{1}{L\cdot \Delta X_i} \cdot \frac{1}{\epsilon_i} \cdot \sum_j M_{ij}^{-1}
    f_{\text{acc}}^{j}(N_{d,j}-N_{b,j}').
\end{equation}

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Statistical uncertainties}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
The uncertainty arising from limited data statistics is estimated and propagated through the unfolding by creating Poisson smeared pseudo-experiments.
The number of events in a bin $X$ is shifted by a random number from the Poisson distribution with the mean $X$
\begin{equation}
    X_\text{shift} = X - \text{Pois}(X).
\end{equation}
Repeating this step 10000 times and evaluating the standard deviation of the toy distributions after the unfolding defines the statistical uncertainty in a given bin.

The same pseudo-experiment approach is also employed to estimate the uncertainty from the finite MC sample size.
The procedure is applied for signal and background samples.
The number of events in each bin is smeared by a Gaussian shift with mean and standard deviation equal
to the yield and the uncertainty of the bin, respectively.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Correlation matrices}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
The covariance matrix is defined as
\begin{equation*}
\text{Cov} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
c_{11} & c_{12} & \cdots & c_{1n} \\
c_{21} & c_{22} & \cdots & c_{2n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
c_{n1} & c_{n2} & \cdots & c_{nn}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
where $c_{ij} = \rho_{ij} \cdot \sigma_i \cdot \sigma_j$ with the correlation coefficient $\rho_{ij}$ and
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_i = \frac{\sigma_i^{\text{up}} + \sigma_i^{\text{down}}}{2}.
\end{equation*}
$\sigma_i^\text{up/down}$ represents the up and down variations in bin $i$. 
The correlation matrix can be calculated by normalizing the covariance matrix
\begin{equation}
    \text{Corr}_{ij} = \frac{\text{Cov}_{ij}}{\sqrt{\text{Cov}_{ii} \times \text{Cov}_{jj}}}
\end{equation}
The statistical correlation matrices for selected quantities are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:unfolding:corrma_stat}.
These matrices serve as a critical diagnostic tool for evaluating the validity and stability of the unfolding procedure.
While the bins are statistically independent prior to unfolding, the unfolding process inherently introduces correlations between them.

A "checkerboard pattern", characterized by strong negative correlations between adjacent bins
in combination with strong positive combinations in next to neighboring bins
indicates an over-fitting of statistical fluctuations.
The regularization is too weak, resulting in an oscillating, physically unrealistic spectrum.

Over-regularization on the other hand manifests itself by large positive correlations across the matrix.
The data is overly smoothed and biased towards the prior model.
Positive correlations across neighboring bins can also indicate that the resolution is not sufficient and
individual bins cannot be resolved.
A coarser binning would be more appropriate in that region.

The total covariance matrix can be calculated as the sum of all systematic and statistical contributions. 
The total correlation matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:unfolding:corrma_full} exhibit strong correlations
over the full range of the distributions.
This is expected, as all systematic uncertainties are assumed to be fully correlated between
adjacent bins.
