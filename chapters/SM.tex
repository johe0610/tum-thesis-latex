\section{The Standard Model of Particle Physics}
\label{sec:SM}

List of aspects to discuss in this section:

Fundamental Constituents

Quarks (6 flavours, 3 generations) and leptons (6, 3 generations), each with an antiparticle
Quarks carry colour charge; leptons do not

Gauge Symmetry and the Lagrangian

The SM is a quantum field theory based on the gauge group $SU(3)_C \times SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$
Each symmetry gives rise to a force via its gauge bosons

The Three Forces

Strong (QCD): SU(3)$_C$, mediated by 8 massless gluons; confinement and asymptotic freedom
Electroweak (EW): Unification of EM and weak under $SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y$; $W^{\pm}, Z^0$, and $\gamma$ as mediators
Note: Gravity is not included in the SM

The Higgs Mechanism

Spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB) via a complex scalar doublet
Gives mass to $W^{\pm}, Z^0$ (and fermions via Yukawa couplings)
Predicts the physical Higgs boson H — discovered at CERN in 2012

Fermion Masses and Mixing

Fermion masses arise from Yukawa couplings after EWSB
Quark mixing described by the CKM matrix; lepton mixing by the PMNS matrix (if neutrinos are massive)
Neutrino masses are not explained by the minimal SM

Key Properties of QCD

Confinement: quarks/gluons not observed freely; only colour-neutral hadrons
Asymptotic freedom: coupling $\alpha_s$ decreases at high energy → perturbative QCD valid at LHC scales
Parton distribution functions (PDFs) needed to describe proton structure

Electroweak Precision and Renormalisability

The SM is renormalisable — divergences can be absorbed order by order in perturbation theory
EW precision observables ($sin^2\theta_W, \rho$-parameter, etc.) have been tested to high accuracy

While the SM has been extremely successful in describing a wide range of phenomena, it is not a complete theory of fundamental interactions.
Known limitations of the SM are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Gravity cannot be incorporated into the SM framework, and a quantum theory of gravity is still lacking.
	\item The SM describes only $5\%$ of the known universe. The other $95\%$ are composed of dark matter and dark energy,
	which are from astrophysical observations, but not accounted for in the SM. No dark matter candidate 
	has been identified up to now.
	\item The SM does not explain the observed matter-antimatter asymmetry in the universe.
	Additional sources of CP violation beyond the SM are required to account for this asymmetry.
	\item The hierarchy problem: the Higgs boson mass is sensitive to quantum corrections, 
	which can drive it to very high values unless fine-tuned cancellations occur. 
	This is considered unnatural and suggests the existence of new physics beyond the SM.
	\item Neutrino masses are not naturally accommodated in the SM. 
	The discovery of neutrino oscillations implies that neutrinos have mass, 
	which requires an extension of the SM, such as the inclusion of right-handed neutrinos or other mechanisms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Top quark physics}


In 1973, Kobayashi and Maskawa proposed the existence of a third generation of quarks to account for CP violation observed in kaon decays as a necessary component of the Standard Model's quark mixing structure~\cite{Kobayashi:1973fv}. 
The third generation up-type quark, the top quark,
was finally discovered by the CDF and D0 collaborations at Fermilab in 1995~\cite{TOP-CDF,TOP-D0}.
Thus, completing the third generation of quarks.

The top quark is the heaviest known elementary particle in the Standard Model, with a mass of approximately 173\GeV. 
This exceptional mass imparts unique properties to the top quark, making its study a cornerstone of the LHC physics program. 
Being heavier than a \Wboson boson, it is the only quark that
decays into a real \Wboson boson and a $b$ quark. The lifetime of about $0.5 \times 10^{-24}$~s~\cite{PDG},
is shorter than the typical hadronization time, which prevents the formation of stable hadronic states.

Therefore, the top quark decays almost exclusively into a
\Wboson boson and a
$b$ quark, while other decay modes are heavily suppressed.
Consequently, final states with a top quark pair (\ttbar) can be categorized by the decay modes of the two
intermediate \Wboson bosons. The relative contributions are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tt_decay_summary}. 
The all-hadronic channel, in which both
\Wboson bosons decay into quarks, possesses the largest branching fraction at approximately 46\%. 
This is followed closely by the single-lepton (or lepton+jets) channel at 44\%, and finally the dilepton channel, 
which has the smallest contribution of 11\%~\cite{PDG}.

\subsection{The WbWb process in the Standard Model}

Many cross section measurements by ATLAS and CMS have measured the \ttbar and single top processes
separately. 
While the ATLAS and CMS collaborations have already measured single and double top-quark production cross-sections in good agreement with theoretical predictions, further precision measurements are essential to probe the nature of the electroweak sector.
This thesis aims at a more inclusive approach by defining the final state as two $b$ quarks and two \Wboson bosons.
Exemplary diagrams of the $WbWb$ production from a resonant top quark pair are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:wbwb_tt:feynman}.
where gluon fusion is the dominant production mode with a relative contribution of 90\% at $\sqrt{s}=13\TeV$~\cite{PDG}.

At NLO QCD, an additional $b$ quark extends the $tW$ process to produce the same $WbWb$ final state,
as illustrated in the diagrams shown in Figure~\ref{fig:wbwb_tW:feynman}.

Contributions from processes with no resonant top quark or no top quark at all
are found to be negligible
The non-resonant $WbWb$ production is not explicitly enhanced in the event selection
and has therefore a negligible contribution to the analysis selections. 

In comparison with measurements
requiring two light charged leptons in the final state, the single-lepton channel provides the opportunity to
measure the \Wboson boson kinematic properties directly, since the hadronically decaying \Wboson boson can be fully
reconstructed. In addition, the larger branching ratio gives access to kinematic regions of the phase space
characterised by more extreme momenta.

The Born level $tW$ production is given by 
\begin{equation}
	b g \rightarrow t W^-
\end{equation}
However, a theoretical defintion of the channel is not straight-forward,
as the $tW$ channel is not well defined beyond LO.
Some Feynman diagram contributing to $tW$ beyond LO can be interpreted as 
\ttbar production in LO. This can be interpreted as an interference between
$tW$ and \ttbar production. The contribution from doubly-resonant diagrams
can become large for $m_{bW}\approx m_t$, the interference must not be neglected.
A possible approach to separate the two processes is presented in~\cite{tWpaper}.

Two alternative schemes are proposed.
The Diagram Removal (DR) removes doubly-resonant diagrams from the calculation of the
$tW$ amplitude. It was shown that the gauge invariance violation does not cause
problems in practical applications.

The Diagram Subtraction (DS) scheme introduces a gauge invariant subtraction term,
modifying the naive $tW$ cross section. Doubly-resonant contributions are removed locally.
