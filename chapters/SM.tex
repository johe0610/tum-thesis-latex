\section{The Standard Model of Particle Physics}
\label{sec:SM}

This section provides a brief introduction to the theoretical framework.
It is by no means meant as a complete comprehensive summary.
It rather gives an overview of the most important concepts.

The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics 
is a Quantum Field Theory that unifies special relativity and quantum mechanics.
It is currently the most comprehensive theory describing matter and anti matter in the universe.
It classifies all known elementary particles into fermions and bosons.
Fermions, which carry half-integer spin, are the basic building blocks of matter,
while bosons, which carry integer spin, act as force carriers to mediate interactions between particles.
It incorporates three fundamental forces: the electromagnetic force, the weak and strong nuclear force.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/ATLAS/SM_particles.png}
    \caption{Caption~\cite{sm_particles_tikz}}
    \label{fig:SM:particles}
\end{figure}

Six flavors of quarks can be arranged in three generations.
Depending on their electrical charge, they are classified as up-type or down-type quarks. In addition to the electrical charge, quarks also carry the color charge $C$ of the strong interaction.
The second category of fermions are the leptons, which are again arranged in 
three generations.
The three electrically charged leptons and the corresponding neutral neutrinos are arranged in three generations as well.
The neutrinos are assumed to be massless in the SM, although the observation of neutrino oscillations implies they must have a small but non-zero mass, requiring an extension of the SM. 
All stable matter surrounding us is made from three fermions: the up- and down-quark and the electron.
An anti-particle exists for every one of the fermions.

The interaction between fermions is described by gauge bosons. The gluon is the mediator of the strong force, described by Quantum Chromo Dynamics (QCD). The associated charge is called color charge in the three kinds red, green and blue.
A crucial property of the strong force is confinement 
which means that there is no free color charge and quarks can only be measured in bound states.
This property forces quarks to ``hadronize'' and to be observed only in color-neutral compound particles.
The strength of the interaction, described by the interaction parameter
$\alpha_S$ decreases when going to higher energies.
This so-called asymptotic freedom justifies the approach of perturbation theory at high energies, which are present at collisions at the Large Hadron Collider.
The SM is renormalizable, which ensures that divergences arising in perturbative calculations are systematically absorbed order by order.
Since the gluon itself carries color charge, self-interaction is allowed, either in a three- or four-gluon vertex. Overall, eight gluons exist.

Electromagnetic and weak interaction can be unified to the electroweak force
with the weak hypercharge ($Y_W$) and the third component of the weak isospin ($T_3$) as associated charges.
The electrical charge can be obtained from a linear combination of the two ($Q=T_3+1/2~Y_W$).
The associated gauge bosons are the charged $W^+$ and $W^-$ bosons, the neutral massive $Z^0$ boson, and the massless photon $\gamma$.
The photon is the mediator of the electromagnetic force, while the $W$ and $Z$ bosons mediate the weak force.
The mixing between the $Z^0$ and $\gamma$ arising from electroweak unification is described by the weak mixing angle $\theta_W$ and is governed by the mechanism of spontaneous symmetry breaking.
The weak force is the only force in the SM that can mediate flavor changing 
process, such as $\beta$ decays.
The mixing of quarks which relates to the transition probability is given by the
Cabibbo - Kobayashi - Maskawa (CKM) matrix.
In addition, the weak force was found to be maximally parity (P) violating.
As a consequence it only couples to left handed particles (and right-handed antiparticles). The combined Charge - Parity (CP) symmetry is not conserved
by the weak interaction. As a consequence, particles may behave slightly differently, when compared to their antiparticles.

Putting the pieces together, the SM is a quantum field theory which can be based on the gauge group $SU(3)_C \times SU(2)_I \times U(1)_Y$.
Each of the symmetries gives rise to a force via its gauge bosons.

The last missing piece of the SM is the scalar Higgs boson with spin 0.
The Higgs Mechanism was introduced by Higgs and Englert and 
explains how particles obtain their mass.
They interact with a scalar Higgs field via Yukawa coupling, where the coupling strength is directly proportional to the particle mass: heavier particles interact more strongly with the Higgs boson.
The vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field is measured to be $246~\GeV$,
and the masses themselves are free parameters of the SM that must be determined experimentally.
The Higgs boson was discovered at the LHC in 2012, around 50 years after
it was theorized, with a mass of approximately $125~\GeV$.
A huge success of the SM and precision physics.

The quark content of the proton is in the simplified picture two up quarks and
one down quark, held together by the strong interaction.
Interestingly, the masses of these quarks account for only a small fraction of the proton mass; the remainder arises from the kinetic and binding energy of the quarks and gluons inside.
Quantum fluctuations also allow the existence of additional quark-antiquark pairs for short timescales.
Gluons, which do not carry mass, can be produced in abundance.
The distribution describing the momentum fraction carried by the partons in the proton is given by Parton Distribution Functions (PDFs).

While the SM has been extremely successful in describing a wide range of phenomena, it is not a complete theory of fundamental interactions.
Known limitations of the SM are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Gravity cannot be incorporated into the SM framework, and a quantum theory of gravity is still lacking.
	\item The SM describes only $5\%$ of the known universe. The other $95\%$ are composed of dark matter and dark energy,
	evidenced by astrophysical observations, but not accounted for in the SM. No dark matter candidate 
	has been identified up to now.
	\item The SM does not explain the observed matter-antimatter asymmetry in the universe.
	Additional sources of CP violation beyond the SM are required to account for this asymmetry.
	\item The Higgs boson mass is sensitive to quantum corrections, 
	which can drive it to very high values unless fine-tuned cancellations occur. 
	Observing the relatively low mass of 125\GeV is
    considered unnatural and suggests the existence of new physics beyond the SM.
    This is known as the hierarchy problem.
	\item Neutrino masses are not naturally accommodated in the SM. 
	The discovery of neutrino oscillations (neutrinos changing their flavor) implies that neutrinos have mass, 
	which requires an extension of the SM, such as the inclusion of right-handed neutrinos or other mechanisms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross section definition}

A cross section $\sigma$ is a measure of the probability of a particular interaction occurring between two colliding particles.
It is given in units of area reflecting the effective interaction region.
The hadronic production cross section of two initial state hadrons $h_1$ and $h_2$ producing a final state with $n$ partons plus additional radiation $X$ can be written as~\cite{black_book_qcd}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \frac{1}{2s}\sum_{a,b}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\text{d}x_a}{x_a}\frac{\text{d}x_b}{x_b}\,f_{a/h_1}(x_a,\mu_F)\, f_{b/h_2}(x_b,\mu_F)\int d\Phi_n \,|\mathcal{M}_{ab\rightarrow n}|^2(\Phi_n;\mu_F,\mu_R)
    \label{eq:xsec:theo}
\end{equation}
where $s$ denotes the hadronic center-of-mass energy squared. 
%Partonic and hadronic center-of-mass energies are related by
%\begin{equation}
%    \hat{s} \equiv x_a x_b s.
%\end{equation}
The LHC collides protons, which are composite particles with partons as constituents.
The parton distribution functions (PDFs) $f_{a/h}(x_a, \mu_F)$ give the probability of finding parton $a$ carrying momentum fraction $x_a$ of the parent hadron at factorization scale $\mu_F$.
The underlying assumption is collinear factorization, stating that cross sections of processes involving initial state hadrons can be factorized 
into process independent PDFs and the matrix element $\mathcal{M}$ with quasi-free partons in the initial state.
%Factorization of the cross section is assumed in this notation.
%Additionally, the transverse momenta of the initial-state partons are neglected.
The PDFs are convoluted with the partonic cross section, which consists of the phase-space integral of the squared matrix element $|\mathcal{M}_{ab \to n}|^2$ over the $n$-particle final state $\text{d}\Phi_n$.

Matrix elements are calculated using the framework of perturbation theory.
The transition amplitude $|\mathcal{M}|^2$ is expanded as a power series in the coupling constant.
Each term in this expansion corresponds to a set of Feynman diagrams, which provide an intuitive 
graphical representation of the underlying partonic interactions. 
A factor is assigned for each propagator, vertex, and external particle, translating the
diagram into a well-defined mathematical expression.
Combining the expressions yields the amplitude for a given process.

The perturbative expansion is organized by the number of vertices appearing in the diagrams,
as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tt:feynman}.
The lowest-order for $gg\rightarrow \ttbar$ has two vertices and is of the order $\mathcal{O}(\alpha_s^2)$.
It is referred to as leading order (LO), since it captures the dominant behavior of the process 
and involves the minimal number of vertices required. 
Every topologically distinct diagram of the same order contributes to the total amplitude.

Higher-order corrections are systematically incorporated by including diagrams with additional 
vertices. Two classes of corrections arise: real corrections, 
in which an additional parton is radiated in the final state, and virtual corrections, 
in which loop diagrams contribute.
Representative diagrams for both are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tt:feynman} for next-to-leading order (NLO) $\mathcal{O}(\alpha_s^3)$
and next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO) $\mathcal{O}(\alpha_s^4)$.
The real and virtual contributions individually may contain infrared and ultraviolet divergences.
When combined, they cancel, yielding finite, physically meaningful predictions. 
The number of Feynman diagrams grows rapidly with perturbative order, making a brute-force evaluation of all contributions computationally impractical. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.5206

Two unphysical scales are introduced in Equation~\eqref{eq:xsec:theo}: the renormalization scale $\mu_R$, at which the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ is evaluated, and the factorization scale $\mu_F$, at which the PDFs are determined. Both scales can be chosen to reflect the hard scale of the process.
In top quark physics, for example, $\mu_R = \mu_F = m_t$ is a common choice, though dynamical scale definitions are also used.
Since the scales are an artifact of the perturbative treatment, physical observables should in principle be independent of their choice.
In practice, residual scale dependence remains due to the truncation of the perturbative series at fixed order.
Each successive order in the perturbative expansion reduces the residual dependence on the scales, improving both the accuracy and the 
precision of the theoretical prediction.
The theoretical uncertainty from missing higher orders is typically estimated by
varying $\mu_R$ and $\mu_F$ by a factor of two around the central value.

\subsection{Top quark physics}

In 1973, Kobayashi and Maskawa proposed the existence of a third generation of quarks to account for CP violation observed in kaon decays as a necessary component of the Standard Model's quark mixing structure~\cite{Kobayashi:1973fv}. 
The third generation up-type quark, the top quark,
was finally discovered by the CDF and D0 collaborations at Fermilab in 1995~\cite{TOP-CDF,TOP-D0}.
Thus, completing the third generation of quarks.

The top quark is the heaviest known elementary particle in the Standard Model, with a mass of approximately 173\GeV. 
This exceptional mass imparts unique properties to the top quark, making its study a cornerstone of the LHC physics program. 
Being heavier than a \Wboson boson, it is the only quark that
decays into a real \Wboson boson and a $b$ quark. The lifetime of about $0.5 \times 10^{-24}$~s~\cite{PDG},
is shorter than the typical hadronization time, which prevents the formation of stable hadronic states.

The interplay of doubly-resonant and singly-resonant diagrams in the $tW$ process can be $tW$ production where \ttbar contributions can be viewed as an NLO correction. The $tW$ channel is not well-defined
beyond LO. Writing the process as
\begin{equation}
    \alpha + \beta \longrightarrow t + W + \delta
    \label{eq:tW_process}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$, $\beta$ are the initial-state partons and $\delta$ is a $b$ quark, the
amplitude is written as the sum of the individual contributions
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta} = \mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}^{(Wt)} + \mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}^{(t\bar{t})}
    \label{eq:tW_amplitude}
\end{equation}
The squared amplitude is then
\begin{equation}
    \left|\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}\right|^{2}
    = \left|\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}^{(Wt)}\right|^{2}
    + 2\,\Re\left\{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}^{(Wt)}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}^{(t\bar{t})\star}\right\}
    + \left|\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\beta}^{(t\bar{t})}\right|^{2}
    \label{eq:tW_amplitude_squared}
\end{equation}
where the three terms correspond to the pure $tW$ contribution, the $tW$--\ttbar
interference, and the pure \ttbar contribution, respectively.
 
The interference term in Eq.~(\ref{eq:tW_amplitude_squared}) cannot be neglected when the invariant mass of the $Wb$ system approaches the top quark mass, $m_{bW}\approx m_t$. The intermediate $t$ propagator goes on-shell, causing a large resonant
enhancement of the cross section.
Reference~\cite{Frixione:2008yi} proposes two schemes to obtain a
usable definition of the $tW$ process.
 
The \textbf{Diagram Removal (DR)} scheme removes all doubly-resonant diagrams from the
$tW$ amplitude, eliminating the \ttbar interference entirely.
This breaks QCD gauge invariance, but a detailed study in~\cite{Frixione:2008yi} shows
the numerical impact to be negligible across the relevant phase space.
 
The \textbf{Diagram Subtraction (DS)} scheme introduces a gauge-invariant local
subtraction term that cancels the doubly-resonant \ttbar contribution to the differential
cross section.
The subtraction term is strongly peaked at $m_{bW}\approx m_t$ and falls steeply away
from the resonance.
Unlike DR, the $tW$--\ttbar interference is preserved, since the subtraction acts at the
level of the squared amplitude.
%To maintain gauge invariance, the $\bar{t}$ amplitude must be evaluated with
%on-shell-reshuffled kinematics, which introduces an additional theoretical uncertainty.
 
%The difference between DR and DS predictions directly measures the size of the
%$tW$--\ttbar interference.
Implementing both schemes in the same framework provides an estimate of the theoretical
systematic uncertainty on the $tW$ signal extraction, which is propagated accordingly in

\subsection{The WbWb process at ATLAS}

The ATLAS collaboration has measured the $WbWb$ process in the dilepton channel,
$pp\rightarrow W^+bW^-\bar{b} \rightarrow \ell^+\nu \ell^-\bar{\nu} b\bar{b}$~\cite{TOPQ-2017-05, atlas:2026wbwb},
combining \ttbar and $tWb$ contributions into a final state with two leptons and two $b$-jets.
To maximize sensitivity to \ttbar$/tW$ interference effects, the analysis exploits the minimax observable
\begin{equation}
    m_{b\ell}^{\text{minimax}} \equiv \min\{\max(m_{b_1\ell_1}, m_{b_2\ell_2}), \max(m_{b_1\ell_2}, m_{b_2\ell_1})\}.
\end{equation}
It is constructed to satisfies the kinematic constraint $m_{b\ell}^{\text{minimax}} < \sqrt{m_t^2 - m_W^2}$ for leading-order doubly-resonant \ttbar events.
The suppression of doubly-resonant contributions above this endpoint enhances the sensitivity to interference in the high-mass tail of the distribution.
The measured differential cross section with two final state $b$ jets, one electron and one oppositely-charged muon is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:wbwb:atlas_dil:minimax} as a function of $m_{b\ell}^{\text{minimax}}$.
\begin{figure}[ht!]
	\centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/WbWb/minimax_wbwb_dilepton.pdf}
    \caption{
    Differential cross section as a function of the $m_{b\ell}^{\text{minimax}}$ observable measured by the ATLAS collaboration~\cite{atlas:2026wbwb}.
    The data are shown as black markers and are compared to several generator predictions.
    The ratio of predictions to the data is shown in the lower panels alongside the statistical
    and systematical measurement uncertainties.
    }
	\label{fig:wbwb:atlas_dil:minimax}
\end{figure}
The data represented by the black points are compared to several theoretical predictions.
The predictions are obtained by merging \ttbar and $tW$ simulation, employing either the DR or DS scheme in the
$tW$ contribution. Discrepancies between the descriptions are visible in the high-mass tail, with the predictions bracketing the data.
The \texttt{bb4l} generator, which computes the full $WbWb$ process at NLO, underestimates the data in that region. The present thesis extends this study to the single-lepton channel, where the inclusive $WbWb$ final state has not been studied yet.

\section{Event generation and simulation}
\label{sec:MC}

The goal of this section is to build a bridge from the theoretical, mathematical definition of particle collisions and the cross section
to the messy, experimentally observable process which incorporates perturbative and non-perturbative effects
as well as a detail real-world simulation of the detector. Interaction of particles traversing the detector material.
This is achieved with the tools of Monte Carlo event generators.
The term Monte Carlo was introduced in reference to the gambling district of Monaco, named Monte Carlo,
and refers to the underlying stochastic nature of physics.
A variety of MC event generators is used in the high energy environment of LHC physics, such as Pythia, Herwig and Sherpa.
This section goes through the step by step process on how to simulate an event.
Starting from the evaluation of the matrix element, over parton shower and hadronization to the detector simulation.

\subsection{Detector simulation}

After the generation and showering of simulated events, the final-state stable particles are propagated through a full simulation of the ATLAS detector. 
This GEANT4 software package~\cite{Agostinelli:2002hh} is used to 
simulate the response of the ATLAS detector, 
converting particle-level truth information into realistic detector-level signals.
The full event reconstruction chain, described in Chapter~\ref{chap:ObjectReco}, is then applied using the same algorithms as for real collision data. This yields simulated events that additionally contain generator and truth-matching information not present in recorded data. The procedure is essential for estimating signal acceptance and efficiency, modeling backgrounds, and validating theoretical predictions against measurement.
