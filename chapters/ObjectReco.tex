\chapter{Object Reconstruction}
\label{chap:ObjectReco}

The raw data output of the ATLAS experiment consists of electrical signals from $\approx 40$ million channels. 
Particle trajectory and energy cluster information are combined in a Particle Flow (PFlow) algorithm to construct objects that serve as input to jet clustering algorithms.
Objects, such as jets, leptons and \ETmiss are reconstructed as high-level objects for
physics analyses. The objects are subsequently calibrated to account for differences
in MC simulation and data and to ensure accurate and precise measurements of their
properties.

This chapter details the reconstruction of tracks, calorimeter cluster and physics objects within the ATLAS detector.

\section{Reconstruction of Tracks, Clusters and Vertices}
\label{sec:ObjRecl:Tracks}
This section provides a concise summary of low level objects that will serve as an input for the reconstruction of physics objects.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Tracks}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ATLAS:DiagramParticles}, charged particles traversing
the various layers of the ATLAS detector produce spatial hits and energy depositions.
The particles are forced on helical trajectories by the
strong magnetic field, enabling the determination of momentum and charge.
The trajectories are reconstructed from ID and MS signals to form tracks.

The first step in the reconstruction of charged particles is the creation of three-dimensional \textit{space points} from neighboring cells in Pixel and SCT that exceed a certain threshold~\cite{PERF-2015-08}.
%The pixel clusters in the barrel are about two pixels in the $r$-$\varphi$ plane and from one to three pixels in the $\eta$ plane.

Sets of three space points form \textit{track seeds} which allow a first crude momentum estimate while maximizing the number of possible combinations.
The candidates are extrapolated by assuming a perfect helical
trajectory in a uniform magnetic field to estimate the distance to beam line. These are also called \textit{transverse impact parameter} $d_0$ and \textit{longitudinal impact parameter} $z_0$.

After applying a first set of momentum and impact parameter requirements, 
a combinatorial Kalman filter~\cite{FRUHWIRTH1987444} is used to
build track candidates from the chosen seeds in an iterative fitting process.
It incorporates additional space-points from the remaining
layers of the Pixel and SCT detectors which are compatible with the preliminary trajectory.
Track candidates created from random collection of space points are rejected to manage the available CPU budget for event reconstruction.

A \textit{track score} is defined evaluating tracks according to their momentum, number of clusters and $\chi^2$ of the fit, where more energetic tracks with an increased number of clusters are ranked higher.
The tracks a processed by an ambiguity solver in descending order of the track score to resolve overlapping space points.

The ambiguity solver rejects all tracks that fail a number of criteria
including kinematic cuts ($\pt > 400 \MeV$, $|\eta|<2.5$) and on the total 
number of clusters and holes. A hole is defined as an intersection of the reconstructed track trajectory with a sensitive detector element that does not contain a matching cluster. The detailed list can be found in~\cite{PERF-2015-08}.

The tracks can be extended in the outer TRT in the next step.
%A two-step approach is used.
An extension algorithm searches for seeded candidates before a second module
processes and evaluates these extensions.
It should be noted, that this is a pure extension, not a combination.
The silicon-only tracks are not modified~\cite{Cornelissen_2008}.

The first step in the reconstruction of muons in the MS~\cite{PERF-2015-10} is to form segments from hit patterns in the muon chambers.
A Hough transform~\cite{ILLINGWORTH198887} is used to search for aligned hits in MDT and trigger chambers.
It speeds up the reconstruction and makes it more robust against misidentification.

A straight line is fitted to the hits in each layer is performed to reconstruct the MDT segments.
Muon track candidates are then built by combining hits from segments in different layers. 
The segment-seeded combinatorial search starts from the middle
layers of the detector where more trigger hits are available,
and is then extended to use the segments from the outer and inner layers.
Finally, a global $\chi^2$ fit is performed to
the hits associated with each track candidate.


\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Calorimeter cluster}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
The reconstruction of particles depositing energy in the calorimeter system is 
based on a three-dimensional topological clustering of individual calorimeter cell signals~\cite{PERF-2014-07}.
The resulting topological cell clusters (\textit{topo-clusters}) carry both, shape and location information.

The cell significance $\varsigma^\text{EM}_\text{cell}$ is defined to control the 
cluster formation
\begin{equation}
    \varsigma^\text{EM}_\text{cell} = \frac{E^\text{EM}_\text{cell}}{\sigma^\text{EM}_\text{noise,cell}}
    \label{eq:ObjReco:topo_threshold},
\end{equation}
where $E^\text{EM}_\text{cell}$ and $\sigma^\text{EM}_\text{noise,cell}$
denote the cell signal and average (expected) noise, respectively.
The algorithm to form topo-clusters starts from seeds with highly significant signal
$|\varsigma^\text{EM}_\text{cell}|>4$ (primary seed threshold). Each of the seed cells forms a \textit{proto cluster}.
All neighboring cells with $|\varsigma^\text{EM}_\text{cell}|>2$ (growth threshold) are collected
iteratively, as well as cells with $|\varsigma^\text{EM}_\text{cell}|>0$ (principal cell filter).
In this case, neighboring cells refers to cells adjacent in
a given sampling layer, as well as cells with partial overlap in adjacent layers.

If a proto cluster has more than one local maximum it is split into separate clusters.
A local maximum is defined as a cell with $E^\text{EM}_\text{cell} > 500~\MeV$
and at least four neighbors, none of them with larger signal.

The resulting proto-cluster is characterized by a core of
seed cells, surrounded by an envelope of cells with less significant signals.
The energy of the cluster is given by the energy sum of the associated cells taking geometrical signal weights into account,
while $\eta$ and $\varphi$ are calculated from the energy-weighted average of the cells. The clusters are assumed to be massless pseudo-particles.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Vertices}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
As mentioned in Section ... , there are multiple proton-proton collisions per bunch crossing.
In order to identify the one collision of interest, the intersection of multiple tracks, referred to as \textit{vertex} is reconstructed~\cite{ATL-PHYS-PUB-2015-026}.
The vertices at the beam line are denoted as \textit{primary vertices}, where the one with the largest sum of squared transverse momenta of associated tracks is labeled the \textit{hard-scatter vertex}.

\textit{Secondary vertices} are displaced with respect to the beam line.
They can be an indication for long-lived particles such as $b$ hadrons, that travel a short distance in the detector before decaying.

%Elliot thesis
Leptons are reconstructed using either tracks or a combination of tracks and topo-
clusters. This discussion focuses on the reconstruction and calibration of electrons

\subsection{Electron Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reco:el}
Offline electron reconstruction starts from dynamic, variable size clusters in the
ECal. The seed clusters need to exceed $E_T > 1~\GeV$ and be matched to a track with at least four space points~\cite{EGAM-2018-01}.
Clusters within $\Delta\eta\times\Delta\varphi = 0.125\times 0.300$
that are matched to the same track tend to represent secondary EM showers originating from the same initial electron and are called \textit{satellite clusters}.
Seed clusters with their associated satellite clusters constitute \textit{superclusters}.

In order to distinguish between \textit{prompt} electrons, that can originate from a $W$ or $Z$ boson decay from the primary vertex, \textit{non-prompt} electrons
that can be produced in hadron decays, photons and low energetic jets, a set of discriminating observables is defined~\cite{PERF-2017-01, EGAM-2018-01}. 
These observables are defined in the region covered by the ECal ($|\eta|<2.47$) and include the number of hits in the ID subsystems and the ration of
energies in the calorimeter system. 
%isolation??
They can be used to define the three working points (WPs) Loose, Medium and Tight
that impose increasingly strict requirements.
%The electron identification efficiency for the three WPs is shown in Figure~\ref{}.
Therefore, the Tight WP exhibits the strongest background suppression, at the cost of a loss in efficiency.

To suppress electrons originating from heavy hadron decays, 
an isolation criteria is applied, checking for detector activity in a cone $(\Delta R < 0.2)$ around the electron.
The \texttt{PLImprovedTight} isolation working point~\cite{web:PLImprovedWPs} is employed.

It is vital to account for performance differences in identification, reconstruction and isolation between data and simulation.
By comparing the respective efficiencies in $Z\rightarrow e^+e^-$ and $J/\psi \rightarrow e^+e^-$ events scale factors (SFs) can be obtained~\cite{EGAM-2018-01}.
The SFs are applied to the MC weights to better describe the data.

The absolute energy scale and the energy resolution is corrected using $Z\rightarrow e^+e^-$ events.
Validation of the calibration is performed with $J/\psi \rightarrow e^+e^-$
and radiative $Z$ processes.
The uncertainties assigned to the calibration are typically 0.05\% for electrons from resonant $Z$ decays~\cite{EGAM-2021-02}.

\subsection{Muon Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reco:mu}
Muons are minimum-ionizing particles.
Their signature in the detector includes tracks in ID and MS.
The characteristic
energy deposits in the calorimeter are also used to account for cases with large energy loss
in the calorimeter system and MS-independent identification.
The information provided by ID, MS and calorimeters is combined, defining five categories of muons~\cite{MUON-2018-03}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Combined (CB) muons are formed with a global refit that uses the independent
    reconstructions in ID and MS.
    \item Inside-out combined (IO) muons are reconstructed using a complementary
    algorithm that extrapolates ID tracks to the MS. Reconstruction can be improved in regions with limited MS coverage, as well as for low-\pt muons, that may be stopped in the MS.
    \item Segment-tagged (ST) muons classify an ID track as a muon if it is associated with
    at least one local track segment in the MDT or CSC.
    \item Calorimeter-tagged (CT) muons match a track in th ID with an energy deposit in the
    calorimeter compatible with a minimum-ionizing particle. The muon \pt threshold has to be increased from 2~\GeV to 5~\GeV due to large background contamination at low \pt.
    \item Extrapolated (ME) muons are reconstructed from MS hits only, adding a loose
    requirement that the origin of the track is compatible with the IP. Thus, extending the coverage to $|\eta|< 2.7$ to exploit the full MS range. 
\end{itemize}
Overlaps between the categories are resolved before producing the collection of muons used in physics analyses.
Similar to the electron identification, a \textit{Loose, Medium} and \textit{Tight} WP is defined for muons to suit the needs of the wide variety of physics analyses involving final states containing muons.
The \textit{Medium} WP, which will be used in the analysis presented in Chapter ...
only accepts CB and IO muons within the ID acceptance $|\eta|<2.5$.
The fraction of CB muons passing the \textit{Medium} WP in \ttbar events exceeds 98\%~\cite{MUON-2018-03}.

Again, a calibration procedure is defined and applied to simulated data to match
the performance measured in real data~\cite{MUON-2022-01}.
Samples of $J/\psi\rightarrow \mu^+\mu^-$ and $Z\rightarrow \mu^+\mu^-$ events
are used to measure the momentum scale and resolution.
The momentum scale and resolution are measured with an uncertainty of 0.5\% and 1.5\% at the Z peak. Charge dependent biases are removed with a dedicated correction.

Systematic uncertainties are defined for variations in the SFs associated with
decay and final state radiation modeling, $J/\psi$ only and $Z$ only fit or statistical uncertainties. The full list is given in~\cite{MUON-2022-01}.

\section{Jet Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reco:jets}

Quarks and gluons cannot be reconstructed as single particles in the detector.
Due to QCD color confinement, they hadronize into collimated sprays of color-neutral particles, which are reconstructed as jets. 

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Particle flow}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
ATLAS constructs jets from Particle Flow (PFlow) objects~\cite{PERF-2015-09}, which optimally combine tracking and calorimeter information. Integrating tracking significantly improves the energy and angular resolution for low-momentum particles, below typical calorimeter noise thresholds.
Furthermore, track-based vertex association allows effective rejection of pile-up contributions.

The PFlow algorithm begins by selecting well-measured tracks and matching them to calorimeter topo-clusters. Overlaps between the momentum and energy measurements made in the inner detector and calorimeters are removed by a cell-based energy subtraction algorithm.
The expected calorimeter energy deposit from the particle that also created the track is estimated from the track momentum and and topo-cluster position.

Since single particles often deposit energy across multiple topo-clusters, the algorithm evaluates shower overlap probabilities and adds relevant clusters where necessary to recover the full shower energy. The expected track energy is then subtracted cell-by-cell from the matched topo-clusters. Any remaining calorimeter energy inconsistent with single-particle shower fluctuations is discarded, yielding a clean list of PFlow objects. 

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Jet clustering}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
The PFlow objects are subsequently clustered into jets using the anti-$k_T$ algorithm~\cite{Cacciari:2008gp}.
A sequential recombination algorithm is defined introducing distances $d_{ij}$ between entities (particles, pseudojets) $i$ and $j$ and $d_{iB}$ between entity $i$ and the beam $(B)$
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    d_{ij} = \text{min}(k_{ti}^{2p},k_{tj}^{2p})\frac{\Delta_{ij}^2}{R^2},\\
    d_{iB} = k_{ti}^{2p}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\Delta^2_{ij} = (y_i - y_j )^2 + (\varphi_i - \varphi_j )^2$ and $k_{ti}$, $y_i$ and $\varphi_i$ are respectively the transverse momentum,
rapidity and azimuth of particle $i$. The jet radius parameter $R$ controls the jet size.

The inclusive sequential recombination algorithm iteratively selects the smallest distance from a set of pairwise $(d_{ij})$ and beam $(d_{iB})$ measures. If the minimum is between entities $(d_{ij})$, they are merged into a single object, 
otherwise $(d_{iB})$, the entity is declared a final jet and removed from the list.
The distances are updated and the procedure repeats until all particles are clustered into jets.

The choice of $p$ defines jet algorithm, where $p=1$ corresponds to the $k_t$~\cite{Ellis_1993}, $p=0$ to the Cambridge/Aachen~\cite{Dokshitzer_1997} and $p = -1$ to the anti-$k_t$ algorithm.
The latter is generally used in ATLAS analyses, as it 
constitutes a simple, IRC safe and soft-resilient jet algorithm, constructing
jets whose shape is not influenced by soft radiation.
The hardest particles act as seeds for the reconstruction reducing sensitivity to pileup and UE effects~\cite{Cacciari:2008gp}.
The analysis presented in Chapter ... employs jets clustered from PFlow objects with the anti$k_t$ algorithm and $R=0.4$. %(\textsc{AntiKt4EMPFlowJets}).
The majority of pile-up jets are rejected using the jet vertex tagger (JVT) tool~\cite{ATLAS-CONF-2014-018}.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{$b$ tagging}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
In order to measure cross sections with the $WbWb$ final state, it is vital to identify jets originating from b quarks.
$b$ jet identification in ATLAS exploits the distinct decay topology of $b$ hadrons.
Their longer lifetimes and larger masses compared to light-flavor or charm hadrons, result in measurable displaced decay vertices. The algorithm relies on ID track information, primarily transverse impact parameters and reconstructed secondary or tertiary vertices. The DL1r~\cite{FTAG-2019-07} algorithm combines multiple low-level taggers into a single discriminant using a deep neural network. 
Further improvements are obtained by exploiting a recurrent neural network in the RNNIP algorithm~\cite{ATL-PHYS-PUB-2017-003}, significantly enhancing discrimination against light-flavor and $c$ jet backgrounds.

Several WPs are defined to balance $b$ jet tagging efficiency against $c$ jet and light flavor rejection. 
For this analysis, the DL1r tagger at WP $77\%$ is employed.
The performance of the low level taggers compared to the combined DL1r tagger is highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:ObjectReco:FtagPerf}.

\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Jet energy scale}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
A multi-step calibration framework is implemented to derive corrections that account for detector response, reconstruction biases, and environmental effects in the \textit{Jet Energy Scale} (JES)~\cite{JETM-2018-05}. The complete calibration chain is summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:ObjectReco:jes_calib}.
Throughout all calibration stages, the jet four-momentum is consistently corrected by scaling jet \pt, energy, and mass.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{figures/ATLAS/jes_calib_chain.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of the several jet energy calibration steps. Each correction is applied to the jet four-momentum~\cite{JETM-2018-05}.}
    \label{fig:ObjectReco:jes_calib}
\end{figure}

To remove excess energy from additional $pp$ interactions (pile-up) from an event, a correction based on \pt density and jet area is derived from MC simulation.
The second step is a residual correction parameterized as a function of 
%the mean number of interactions per bunch crossing
$\mu$ and the number of reconstructed primary vertices in the event ($N_{PV}$). 

The absolute JES calibration corrects the 4-momentum of the jets in bins of \pt and $\eta$ to account for energy losses in passive material and biases in the reconstruction.
Reconstructed jets are geometrically matched to truth jets to derive the calibration from
simulations of dĳet events.

The global sequential calibration removes the dependence on observables constructed using information from the tracking, calorimeter, and muon systems.
The discrepancies originate from the different average energy distributions and constituent multiplicities of quark or gluon-initiated jets.
It improves the jet resolution without changing the average jet energy response.

All these calibrations are applied to both data and MC
simulation. 

To correct for remaining differences between data and MC simulation
a three staged in-situ calibration is applied to the data only.
The differences are caused by an imperfect simulation of both detector materials and
underlying physics processes, including pile-up, jet formation or interaction of particles with the detector material.

The first step corrects the energy scale of forward jets ($0.8 < |\eta_\text{det}| < 4.5$) to match those of central jets ($|\eta_\text{det}| < 0.8)$.
Subsequently, the hadronic recoil in an event is balanced against well calibrated $Z$ bosons or photons. The same concept is applied in the last step, where a system of well-calibrated low-\pt jets is used to calibrate single high-\pt jets. %Multi Jet Balance (MJB)

An extensive list of systematic uncertainties with 125 components is defined for the JES~\cite{JETM-2018-05} including uncertainties on the pile-up and flavor composition modeling, as well as dependence on the reference objects used in the calibration.
Several sources can be combined, taking the respective correlations between the JES uncertainties of two jets at different \pt and $\eta$ into account.
The \verb|CategoryReduction| scheme~\cite{web:JetSystematics} is used in the measurement. It provides roughly 30 independent variations that parametrize the uncertainties in the JES.

The JES systematic uncertainty is one of the dominating uncertainties for jet based observables.
For central jets it ranges from $1\%$ up to $5\%$ close to the \pt threshold at 20~\GeV~\cite{JETM-2018-05}. 


\vspace{.5cm}
\noindent
\textbf{Jet energy resolution}
\vspace{.5cm}

\noindent
In addition to a precise understanding of the JES and the associated uncertainties, 
the \textit{Jet Energy Resolution} (JER).
In order to measure the JER, two jets need to be balanced against each other in a well-defined dijet system. It can be parametrized using a functional form with three independent terms  related to noise ($N$), stochastic effects ($S$) and a constant offset ($C$)~\cite{JETM-2018-05}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\sigma(\pt)}{\pt} = \frac{N}{\pt} \oplus \frac{S}{\sqrt{\pt}} \oplus C
\end{equation}
The noise term accounts for fluctuations from electronics and pile-up.
It is independent of the energy deposited in the calorimeters and is expected to be significant for low energetic jets. Direct estimations can be obtained with the \textit{random cone} method described in Reference~\cite{JETM-2018-05}.
The stochastic term scales with $1/\sqrt{\pt}$ and accounts for fluctuations in the amount of energy deposited. It represents the limiting term in the resolution.
The dominating term at high momenta $(\pt > 400~\GeV)$ is a constant contribution corresponding
to fluctuations in the starting point of the hadron shower as well as a non-uniform response
across the calorimeter and and energy depositions in the passive material.

%The relative jet energy resolution ranges from $(24\pm 1.5)~\%$ at 20~\GeV to $(6\pm 0.5)~\%$ at 300~\GeV.
A smearing procedure is introduced to ensure that the resolution of the jet energy scale in simulation matches that in data~\cite{JETM-2018-05}.
The uncertainty in the jet energy resolution is assessed using the \verb|FullJER| scheme~\cite{web:JetSystematics} providing 13 independent variations.

\section{MET Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reco:met}

In an ideal collision, the protons collide head-on along the beam line with a vanishing momentum component in the transverse plane.
An imbalance in the final state transverse momentum of an event therefore indicates particles that do not leave a measurable signals in the detector.
The missing transverse energy \ETmiss is typically associated with neutrinos
in physics analyses that do not consider BSM processes.

Two contributions characterize the reconstructed \ETmiss~\cite{JETM-2020-03}.
The fully reconstructed and calibrated leptons, photons and jets enter, being part of the
\textit{hard event}. The second contribution comes from  \textit{soft event} signals
consisting of calibrated tracks associated with the hard scatter vertex, that are not associated to any other reconstructed objects.
\ETmiss is constructed from the transverse momentum vectors of the various contribution as the negative vector sum.
%The \textit{signal ambiguity resolver} ensures that there is no double counting of the same signal when reconstructing \ETmiss by removing overlapping objects.

Systematic uncertainties on
\ETmiss are driven by the energy scale and resolution of all objects contributing to the soft and hard terms. The hard term composition is analysis-specific and uncertainties are extracted per object type using official ATLAS recommendations. 
Uncertainties for identical object types are treated as fully correlated, while those for different object types are assumed uncorrelated, reflecting the independent reconstruction and calibration chains.

\section{Overlap removal}
\label{sec:ObjRec:OR}

This section was copied from the int note and still needs to be modified.

To mitigate double counting of energy deposits arising from objects that are reconstructed twice (e.g., an electron also forming a jet),
ambiguous object are resolved using an overlap removal procedure~\cite{Adams:1700874}.
Loose object definitions listed in Tables~\ref{tab:ObjReco:Electrons} and~\ref{tab:ObjReco:Muons} are applied for electrons, muons, and jets for this step.
The overlap removal algorithm resolves overlaps in the following order:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Remove PFlow jets that are actually muons.
    \item Muons overlapping with electrons that leaves a sufficiently high energy deposit
    in the calorimeter and share the same ID track. Any electron sharing a track with the remaining muons is removed.
    \item Jets within $\Delta\eta< 0.2$ of an electron are removed. Electrons within $\Delta\eta< 0.4$ of the remaining jets are discarded.
    \item muon-jet: Jets within $\Delta\eta< 0.2$ of a muon are removed if they feature less than three tracks or fulfill 
    $p_{T,\mu} / p_{T,\text{jet}} > 0.5$ and $p_{T,\mu} / \Sigma p_{T,\text{tracks}} > 0.7$ are removed. Muons overlapping with $\Delta\eta< 0.4$ of the remaining jets are removed.
\end{itemize}



\section{Definition of physics objects on particle level}
\label{sec:ObjReco:Part}
The aim of this section is to provide definitions of physics objects, such as leptons, jets and missing momentum, that allow for a direct comparison of theoretical and experimental results.
The ATLAS collaboration provides recommendations~\cite{ATL-PHYS-PUB-2015-013} for particle level definitions with a minimal model dependence.
They should be accurate, unambiguous and allow the comparison of predictions with measurement results between experiments without extensive prior experimental knowledge.
The specified particle level objects are as close to experimentally reconstructed objects as possible, 
avoiding large extrapolations to regions outside of the detector acceptance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item All \textbf{stable particles}, defined by a lifetime $c\tau_0 > 10$ mm within the angular acceptance $|\eta| < 5$ are considered.
    \item \textbf{Prompt leptons} are generated final-state neutrinos, electrons and muons not originating from hadron decays.
    Charged leptons from subsequent $\tau$ decays $(W\rightarrow \tau\nu_\tau \rightarrow \ell\nu_\ell\nu_\tau)$ are therefore also categorized as prompt.
    To take QED FSR into account, the charged leptons are \textit{dressed} by clustering all photons within a cone around the \textit{bare} lepton (after simulating QED FSR) with the anti-$k_t$ algorithm. The clustering distance parameter is set to $R=0.1$.
    \item \textbf{Jets} are build in a subsequent step by collecting all stable particles apart from the identified prompt dressed leptons and prompt photons. The anti-$k_t$ algorithm with $R=0.4$ is employed for the jet building.
    The jet is labeled as a $b$ jet if a $b$ hadron with $p_T>5\GeV$ is found within $\Delta R < 0.3$ around the jet axis. Otherwise it is labeled as a \textit{light} jet. 
    No jet-lepton overlap removal is applied.
    \item \textbf{Missing transverse momentum} $\ETmiss$
    corresponds to the transverse vector sum of all neutrinos not from hadron decays.
\end{itemize}
